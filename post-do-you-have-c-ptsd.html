Trauma & EMDR

Do You Have C-PTSD?

By Cristina Ally, LMHC · Flourish Counseling Co. · June 4, 2020

One of the most common ways this conversation starts is a version of the same sentence: "I don't really have trauma. Nothing happened to me. It was just how things always were."

That sentence usually matters far more than the person saying it realizes. "It was just how things always were" is not a reason to rule trauma out. For a lot of people, it is the reason to look closer.

What PTSD describes, and what it leaves out

Most of us learned about post-traumatic stress from stories with a clear before and after. A crash. An assault. A deployment. Something identifiable happened on a particular day, and afterward the nervous system kept behaving as though the danger had never ended. That model is real, and for many people it fits exactly.

But it leaves out a large group of survivors whose experience never had a date on it. Years in a house where affection was conditional and moods arrived like weather. A marriage where you learned to read a face before you read a room. A childhood in which the person you needed for safety was the same person you needed safety from. A decade of caregiving that quietly rearranged your whole life.

The term that has grown up around this pattern is Complex PTSD, usually shortened to C-PTSD. It describes trauma that was prolonged, repeated, and difficult or impossible to escape. Just as importantly, it names the fact that this kind of exposure tends to shape more than memory. It shapes how you regulate emotion, how you see yourself, and how you do closeness.

Is C-PTSD a real diagnosis? It depends which book you open

This is where I try to be straight with people, because the internet often is not.

The World Health Organization's ICD-11, the classification system used across much of the world, does list Complex Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder as its own diagnosis. It requires the core features of PTSD plus what the ICD calls disturbances in self-organization: persistent difficulty regulating emotion, a persistently negative and diminished view of yourself, and persistent difficulty feeling close to other people. You can read the entry yourself in the ICD-11 browser.

The DSM-5-TR, which is the manual most American clinicians and insurers work from, does not. As the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs' National Center for PTSD explains, rather than adding a separate category, "the diagnosis of PTSD was modified and expanded to capture some of the symptoms of CPTSD" (National Center for PTSD, Complex PTSD).

So one well-informed professional may tell you C-PTSD is a formal diagnosis, and another may tell you it is not, and neither of them is lying to you. They are reading different manuals.

What matters clinically is smaller and more useful than the label argument. Was the harm ongoing rather than single-incident? Did it happen inside a relationship you depended on? Was there a long stretch with no exit? If the answer is yes, your treatment should be paced for that, whatever we end up writing on the paperwork.

What it tends to look like from the inside

These are the patterns that come up most often. They are not a checklist and they are not a diagnosis, but they are worth recognizing.

  • Emotion that arrives at full volume. There is no dimmer switch. A small comment lands like an accusation, and the recovery from it takes hours rather than minutes.
  • A self-concept built out of the harm. Not "that was a cruel thing someone did to me," but "there is something fundamentally wrong with me." The shame feels like information rather than a symptom.
  • Closeness that feels like exposure. You may want connection badly and also find that being known sets off an alarm. Many people oscillate between the two and conclude they are simply bad at relationships.
  • Vigilance you have mistaken for personality. Reading the room is not a quirk you were born with. It was a skill, and you were very good at it because you had to be.
  • Memory that is patchy rather than cinematic. People expect trauma memory to look like a film clip. Often it is more like weather: a body sensation, a smell, a sudden mood with no story attached, whole years that are thin.
  • A long history of near-misses with treatment. Several rounds of therapy that helped a little. Anxiety and depression labels that were accurate but felt incomplete. The sense that you got good at describing the problem without the problem ever moving.

Why it gets missed

Two reasons, mostly. The first is that the visible symptoms of C-PTSD overlap heavily with anxiety, depression, ADHD, and some personality-disorder presentations. Treat the surface and you will get partial relief, which is genuinely worth having, and which can also stall you for years.

The second is that survivors of ongoing harm are often the last people to call it harm. If it started early enough, it is not an event you compare yourself to. It is the baseline. It is common for someone to describe years of frightening treatment in a level, reasonable voice, and then apologize for raising something so ordinary.

What treatment actually looks like

The single most common fear about getting help is that it will mean narrating the worst years of your life in detail to a stranger. That is not how I work, and it is not what the evidence-based trauma therapies require.

Work here tends to move in three stages, and the first one is not the story.

Stabilize first. Before anything is processed, you need somewhere to stand. That means predictable sleep and food, a few regulation skills that work in your actual life rather than in a quiet room, and enough trust between us that you can say "stop" and be taken seriously the first time. For some people this stage is short. For people carrying years of it, it is the real work for a while, and rushing it is how therapy goes badly.

Resolve, without reliving. I am trained in Accelerated Resolution Therapy and EMDR. All three are structured approaches designed to help the brain reprocess distressing material so that it stops behaving like a present-tense emergency. They involve far less retelling than people expect. Trauma-focused therapies of this family are recommended in the American Psychological Association's clinical practice guideline for PTSD and the VA and Department of Defense guideline. I will not promise you a timeline or an outcome, because nobody honest can.

Grow forward. When the past is quieter, there is usually a practical question left over: who are you when you are not managing a threat? That stage is often the most interesting one. For material that is hard to put into words, I also use sand tray, which gives the work somewhere to go when language runs out.

Please read this part. Nothing on this page is a diagnostic tool. It is a description of a pattern, written to help you decide whether to ask someone qualified. If any of it sounds like you, the next step is an assessment with a licensed clinician, not a conclusion drawn from an article.

If you are in crisis or thinking about harming yourself, call or text 988 in the United States to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

One last thing

If you have spent years believing your history does not qualify as trauma because nobody filmed it and nothing made the news, I would gently push back. The nervous system does not check whether an event was dramatic enough. It checks whether you were safe, and for how long, and whether anyone came.

You are not what happened to you. With the right support, and at a pace you set, the person underneath it can finally have some room.
