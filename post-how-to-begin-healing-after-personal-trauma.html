Trauma & EMDR

How to Begin Healing After Trauma

By Cristina Ally, LMHC · Flourish Counseling Co. · July 22, 2021

"Where do I even start?"

It is the question that comes up in first sessions more than any other, and it is almost always asked with a particular kind of exhaustion behind it. Not "tell me the technique." More like "I have been carrying this for a while and I have run out of ideas."

Here is the honest answer, and it is not the one most people expect: you do not start with the story.

Why the first stage is not the story

There is a widespread assumption that healing from trauma means sitting down and telling someone the whole thing, in order, in detail, and that the telling is the medicine. It is a reasonable assumption. It is also the reason a lot of people either avoid therapy entirely or leave it feeling worse.

The framework most trauma clinicians work from, articulated by the psychiatrist Judith Herman in her book Trauma and Recovery, is three-stage: safety and stabilization first, then remembrance and mourning, then reconnection. That order is not a formality. Processing difficult material asks your system to go toward something hard and then come back. If the coming-back part is not established yet, going toward it is just getting hurt again on a schedule.

So the first steps are almost boring. That is by design, and I would rather tell you that up front than have you feel like the real work is being withheld.

First steps that actually help

1. Get the physical basics back before anything else

Sleep, food, water, some movement, and roughly consistent timing for all of it. I know how this sounds when what you want is relief from something enormous. But a sleep-deprived, under-fed nervous system cannot regulate, and everything else on this list is harder in that state. If you do one thing this week, make it a consistent bedtime. Not a perfect one. A consistent one.

2. Accept what you feel without ranking it

Fear, rage, numbness, relief, guilt, and boredom are all normal after something traumatic, and they often arrive in the wrong order or at the same time. People frequently get stuck not on the feeling itself but on the judgment stacked on top of it: I shouldn't still be this angry, I should be over this, other people have had it worse.

That comparison is one of the most reliably useless thoughts in the recovery process. Your nervous system did not benchmark your experience against anyone else's before it responded. Feelings that are allowed to exist tend to move. Feelings that have to be justified first tend to stay.

3. Tell one person, and you do not have to tell them everything

Isolation is one of the most common accelerants. It is also completely understandable: talking feels like it will make it real, or like it will burden someone, or like you will not be able to stop once you start.

You can name it without narrating it. "Something happened a while ago that I'm still dealing with, and I'm not ready to talk about the details, but I didn't want to keep pretending I'm fine" is a complete sentence. Ask for company rather than conversation if that is what you have capacity for. Sitting in the same room while someone cooks dinner is a real intervention, and it asks almost nothing of you.

4. Take back one small piece of control

Trauma is, at its core, an experience of having no say. A lot of what lingers afterward is the helplessness rather than the event. The antidote is not a grand gesture, it is repeated small evidence that your actions still change things.

Rearrange a room so you can see the door. Decide what you are doing on Saturday and then do it. Help a neighbor with something concrete. Pick the restaurant. These sound trivial written down. They are not trivial to a system that has concluded that choices do not matter.

5. Notice what is already working

You have been getting through this somehow. Something in your week is helping, even if it is small and even if you would not have chosen it: a particular walk, a specific friend, music in the car, the dog. Trauma recovery has a strong tendency to focus entirely on the damage. Naming the two or three things that already steady you gives you something to lean on deliberately instead of by accident.

What to be careful with

  • Deep-diving the memory on your own. Deliberately going back through the worst of it alone, late at night, looking for insight, is not processing. It is usually rehearsal, and it tends to strengthen the very thing you are trying to loosen.
  • Timelines. There is no calendar for this and anyone who gives you one is guessing. Recovery is rarely linear. A bad week after three good ones is a bad week, not a relapse.
  • Drinking to sleep. Extremely common, entirely understandable, and it reliably makes the sleep worse and the mornings harder. If this is already a pattern, it is worth naming to someone rather than managing alone. The SAMHSA National Helpline is free, confidential, and open all day and night at 1-800-662-4357.
  • Waiting until you are worse. A lot of people hold off on getting help because they are still functioning. Functioning is not the threshold. Cost is. If you are spending a large part of your energy managing this, that is enough.

When it is time to bring in a professional

There is no bright line, but these are the signals I would take seriously: the symptoms have not eased at all after several weeks, or they are getting worse; you are avoiding more and more places, people, or conversations in order to stay steady; sleep has not recovered; your relationships or your work are visibly affected; or you are using something to get through the evenings. The National Institute of Mental Health's overview of PTSD is a solid, non-alarming place to read more.

What I want you to know about the work itself is that it does not require you to relive anything. I am trained in Accelerated Resolution Therapy and EMDR. These are structured approaches that help the brain reprocess a distressing memory so it stops operating as a present-tense emergency, and they involve considerably less retelling than most people fear. Trauma-focused therapies of this kind are recommended in the American Psychological Association's PTSD guideline and the VA and Department of Defense clinical practice guideline. For what is hard to put into words at all, I also use sand tray.

And we would still start with stabilization. You set the pace. Nothing happens before you are ready, and "not today" is always an acceptable answer in my office.

If you are in crisis or having thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. If you are in immediate danger, call 911. This article is general information, not clinical advice, and it is not a substitute for an assessment with a licensed clinician.

Start smaller than you think

If the whole of this feels like too much, pick one line from the list above and do only that this week. Consistent bedtime. One honest sentence to one person. One decision you make and follow through on.

Healing after trauma is not a single brave act. It is a long series of very small ones, and the first few are allowed to be unimpressive.
