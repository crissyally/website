Meditation and Trauma: What Helps, What Backfires

By Cristina Ally, LMHC · Flourish Counseling Co. · May 30, 2020

Someone tells you to try meditation. You download an app, sit down, close your eyes, and within ninety seconds your heart is going, your chest is tight, and you are further from calm than when you started. You conclude, reasonably, that you are bad at meditating.

You are not bad at meditating. You are a person with a trauma history who was handed a general-purpose instruction and no adjustments.

Breath and grounding work have a real place in trauma therapy, and I think they are genuinely valuable. I also think the way meditation gets recommended to trauma survivors is careless, and that the careless version costs people their confidence. So this is the honest version: why it can backfire, what to change, and where to start.

Why the standard instructions can go wrong

Most beginner meditation asks for three things at once: close your eyes, sit still, and turn your attention inward toward your breath and body.

For someone whose nervous system has spent years treating the inside of their own body as a source of alarm, those three instructions are not neutral. Closing your eyes removes your ability to check the room. Sitting still removes the option of moving, which for some survivors is exactly the impulse that got interrupted during the original event. And turning attention inward, with nothing else to hold onto, can hand you straight to the sensations you have been working hard to stay ahead of.

This is not a fringe concern. The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health notes plainly in its overview of meditation and mindfulness that these practices are generally considered safe for healthy people but that adverse experiences have been reported, and that people with existing mental health conditions should talk with a health care provider before starting. The researcher and teacher David Treleaven has built an entire body of work, trauma-sensitive mindfulness, around exactly this gap between the standard instruction and the traumatized nervous system.

None of that means meditation is off the table. It means the default settings are wrong for you, and the settings are adjustable.

The adjustments that actually matter

Here is what I change when I teach this work to trauma clients. Most of it is unglamorous.

  • Eyes open. Let your gaze rest low and soft on a fixed point on the floor. You keep your peripheral awareness of the room, which means your system does not have to guess whether it is safe.
  • Orient before you go inward. Before any breath work, slowly turn your head and let your eyes land on five things in the room. Name them silently. This is not a warm-up, it is the point: you are giving your nervous system current evidence about where you are.
  • Pick a safe anchor. The breath is the standard anchor, and for a lot of survivors it is the worst possible one, because chest tightness is part of the symptom. Try an external anchor instead. The weight of your feet on the floor. The sound of the air conditioning. The texture of the chair arm under your thumb.
  • Keep it genuinely short. Two minutes done regularly beats twenty minutes attempted once and abandoned. I would rather you finish early and intact than white-knuckle a timer.
  • Build in an exit ramp. Decide before you begin what you will do if it gets to be too much: open your eyes fully, stand up, get water, look out a window. Knowing you can stop is part of what makes it possible to start. Stopping is not failing the exercise.
  • Movement counts. Walking, stretching, and washing dishes with your full attention are all legitimate practices. Stillness is not a requirement, it is one option among several.

A short practice to start with

Try this once, today, and give it three minutes.

  1. Sit somewhere with your back supported and both feet flat on the floor. Keep your eyes open, gaze low and soft.
  2. Slowly turn your head to the left, then to the right, letting your eyes actually land on things rather than sweeping past them. Take your time. Notice if you sigh or if your shoulders drop, and do not manufacture it if you don't.
  3. Bring your attention to the pressure of your feet against the floor. Not your breath yet. Just the contact, and the weight.
  4. Now let your breathing be slightly slower than it wants to be, with the out-breath a little longer than the in-breath. Do not force the inhale deep. The long exhale is doing the work.
  5. Stay there for a minute or two. When you finish, look around the room again and notice one thing you had not registered before.

If you want something more structured, I wrote a step-by-step guide to voo breathing, a specific exercise from Peter Levine's work that pairs a long exhale with a low tone you can feel vibrate in your chest. It is one of the few practices worth teaching almost universally, because the sound gives you something to do with your attention that is not scanning your own body for danger.

What meditation is for, and what it is not for

I want to be careful here, because the article this page replaced was not.

Meditation is a regulation skill. It can widen the window in which you feel like yourself, it can shorten the recovery time after something rattles you, and it can make the difference between a hard evening and a lost weekend. It also tends to make trauma treatment go better, because processing work asks you to touch difficult material and then come back, and coming back is a skill you can practice in advance.

Meditation is not a replacement for trauma treatment, and it is not a medication decision. Whether you take psychiatric medication, and whether you continue it, is a conversation between you and your prescriber. Nobody should be adjusting that based on a blog post, mine included.

It is also not a test you can fail. If a practice makes you feel worse, that is useful information about the practice, not a verdict on you. Change one variable, usually the anchor or the length, and try again. If it keeps going badly, bring it to your therapist and work on it together rather than alone.

I always tell clients to learn a coping skill before they need it. We learn to swim when we are calm, not while we are going under. A two-minute practice you have done fifty times on ordinary days is a tool. The same practice attempted for the first time in the middle of a hard night is just one more thing that isn't working.

If the hard part keeps coming back

Grounding skills help you manage the present. They do not, on their own, resolve the material that keeps generating the alarm. When the same memory, the same body sensation, or the same dread keeps returning no matter how well you regulate, that is usually a sign the memory itself needs attention rather than better coping.

That is the work I do. I am trained in Accelerated Resolution Therapy and EMDR, both of which are structured ways of helping the brain reprocess distressing memories without requiring you to narrate them in detail. Approaches in this family are recommended in the American Psychological Association's PTSD guideline.

If you would like to start somewhere gentler than that, start with three minutes and your feet on the floor. It counts.
