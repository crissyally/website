When to Worry: Signs of Trauma in Someone You Love

By Cristina Ally, LMHC · Flourish Counseling Co. · October 22, 2020

Many of the people who reach out about trauma are not asking for themselves.

They are asking about a spouse who has not been the same since the accident. A sister who went quiet after the divorce and has stayed quiet. A friend who used to call every week and now answers texts three days later with a single word. They tend to open with some version of the same worry: am I overreacting, or is something actually wrong?

It is a hard question, because trauma rarely announces itself. What you notice is not the trauma. It is the shape someone's life has quietly changed into around it.

What to look for

None of these on its own means much. What matters is a cluster of them, a change from how this person used to be, and persistence over weeks rather than days.

The past keeps arriving in the present

Nightmares that recur. Going somewhere else mid-sentence. A reaction to a sound, a smell, or a date on the calendar that seems out of proportion to anyone who does not know what it connects to. You may notice them flinch at something you did not even register.

The map of their life is shrinking

This is the one most people miss, because avoidance looks like preference. They take the longer route now. They stopped going to that restaurant. They will not watch certain films. They changed gyms, or shifts, or churches, and the reason given was practical and slightly thin. Each individual choice is unremarkable. Laid end to end over a year, it is a map with pieces cut out of it.

They are on alert, all the time

Sitting facing the door. Startling badly at a dropped pan. Checking locks more than once. Difficulty settling in a crowd. Trouble falling asleep, or waking at three and staying awake. The body has concluded that vigilance is still required and has not been persuaded otherwise.

Anger that does not match the trigger

Irritability and sudden anger are among the most misread signs of trauma, because they are the least sympathetic. It is easy to experience someone's temper as a character problem. Often it is a nervous system with no margin left, where a small demand lands on a system that is already at capacity.

They have gone flat

Not sad, exactly. Absent. Present in the room and not really in it. Things that used to matter do not seem to. This is numbing, and it often follows a period of intense distress, which can make it look like improvement from the outside. It usually is not.

The volume is being turned down chemically

More drinking than there used to be, and more of it alone or specifically at night. Anything that makes the evening survivable becomes load-bearing quickly. The National Center for PTSD has a straightforward explanation of why post-traumatic stress and substance use travel together so often.

The way they talk about themselves has changed

Listen for shame rather than sadness. "I should have done something." "I'm just difficult now." "You'd be better off without me." Self-blame is extremely common after trauma, including in situations where the person plainly had no control, and it is one of the clearest signals that this is not simply a rough patch.

The National Institute of Mental Health's overview of PTSD is a good, calm reference if you want to read further without falling into the worst corners of the internet.

What to say

You do not need the right words. You need durable ones. Say something specific and low-pressure, and then let it sit.

  • "I've noticed you haven't been sleeping. I'm not trying to fix it. I just wanted you to know I noticed."
  • "You don't have to tell me anything. I'd just like to be around more, if that's okay."
  • "I'm not going anywhere. Whenever you want to talk, or not talk, I'm here."

And things worth avoiding, all of which are said with love and land badly:

  • "It's been a year." There is no schedule for this, and implying there is one usually produces silence rather than progress.
  • "At least..." Any sentence beginning this way is a comparison, and comparison is how people learn to stop mentioning things.
  • "Have you tried just not thinking about it?" They have. That is the problem.
  • Pressing for details. Curiosity is natural and it is not the same as support. Let them decide what to say and when.

What you can actually do

Support is mostly logistics and patience rather than conversation.

  • Remove the friction. Finding a therapist is genuinely hard when you are depleted. Offer to sit with them while they look, or to make the call, or to drive them to a first appointment and wait in the car. In Florida you can verify any clinician's license through the Department of Health license lookup, which is a useful and concrete thing to do for someone.
  • Offer specifics, not open offers. "Let me know if you need anything" puts the work back on them. "I'm bringing dinner Thursday, is six alright?" does not.
  • Keep inviting. Expect to be declined, and invite again anyway. Being still asked matters a great deal, even when the answer is no.
  • Stay steady when it is hard. Withdrawal and irritability tend to push people away, which confirms the belief that they are a burden. Not taking it personally is a real contribution.
  • Look after yourself too. Supporting someone through this is genuinely tiring, and you are allowed to have limits and your own support. The VA's guide on helping a family member who has PTSD is written for exactly this position.

Where your job ends

You are not their therapist, and trying to be will cost you both. You cannot argue someone out of a trauma response, you cannot make them go, and their recovery is not a measure of how good a partner or sibling or friend you are. What you can do is stay reachable and keep the door open.

Adults get to decline help. That is genuinely hard to sit with when you can see what it is costing them. Being the person who is still there in six months is not a small thing.

When not to wait. If they talk about ending their life, give away possessions, or say goodbye in a way that feels final, take it seriously and act now. Call or text 988 to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, which also advises people who are worried about someone else. If they are in immediate danger, call 911. For substance use, the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357 is free, confidential, and staffed around the clock.

This article is general information, not clinical advice, and it is not a substitute for an assessment by a licensed clinician.

If they are ready

My practice in Winter Park works with adults on exactly this. I am trained in Accelerated Resolution Therapy and EMDR, which are structured approaches to helping the brain reprocess traumatic memories without requiring someone to retell the story in detail. That last part is worth passing along, because the fear of having to relive it is what keeps a lot of people out of the chair.

And if they are not ready, that is alright. You can hold the information until they are.

If this resonates and you'd like support, you don't have to do it alone. Request an appointment whenever you're ready.
